The UNESCO 1970 Convention was the first international treaty in the fight against the illicit trafficking of cultural property.

The UNESCO 1970 Convention gives a central role to prevention, through inventories and export certificates.

Illicit trafficking is a crime. The UNESCO 1970 Convention is the cornerstone instrument for the protection of movable cultural property.

The UNESCO 1970 Convention is based on three pillars: Prevention, Return and Restitution, and International Cooperation.

More than 145 States are parties to the UNESCO 1970 Convention.

States Parties of the UNESCO 1970 Convention must set up cultural property inventories, educational campaigns and prevention tools.

UNESCO is the only UN specialised agency with a dedicated mandate in the field of culture.

The 1970 UNESCO Convention calls on States to help safeguard the cultural heritage of other States when it is in jeopardy.

The UN Security Council recognised in 2017 that cultural heritage threats are security risks — there is a global duty to protect it.

Francis Kéré is a renowned Burkinabè-German architect who designs sustainable buildings with local and innovative techniques.

The UNESCO Virtual Museum's architecture is inspired by the baobab tree — a symbol of resilience and central for the life of many African communities.

Francis Kéré, architect of the UNESCO Virtual Museum, is the first African Laureate of the prestigious Pritzker Prize, awarded in 2022.

The architecture of the UNESCO Virtual Museum is also inspired by the Guggenheim Museum in New York, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The galleries of the UNESCO Virtual Museum have been designed as a continuous journey, placing stolen objects back in their original context.

Francis Kéré is celebrated for designs that actively involve and empower local communities.

In the Return and Restitution Room, trees, natural light and open sky symbolise life and reappropriation.

Francis Kéré designed the UNESCO Virtual Museum as a place of gathering, where cultural heritage acts as the roots that connect and nourish communities.

The architecture of the museum shows heritage as a living network, linking objects, stories and traditions, all at risk from trafficking and loss.

The design of the entrance hall represents the continents of the world, all connected in the protection of our common heritage.

The UNESCO Virtual Museum highlights the value of cultural heritage as a key element of our identity and cultural rights.

The museum tells the stories of stolen objects, helping educate and engage visitors while raising public awareness.

The UNESCO Virtual Museum is a response to calls for collective action to raise public awareness about illicit trafficking.

The UNESCO Virtual Museum is the world's first virtual museum at a global scale dedicated to stolen cultural property.

The stolen objects in the museum represent a significant loss to the cultural heritage of the submitting States.

The Stolen Objects Gallery will gradually empty as items are recovered, whilst the Return and Restitution Room grows.

The museum shares the stories of stolen objects and the voices of their communities of origin.

The UNESCO Virtual Museum is a platform to federate all stakeholders engaged in fighting illicit trafficking.

The galleries

A museum that grows like a baobab

Designed with Pritzker Prize laureate Francis Kéré, the museum unfolds upward through a helical promenade inspired by the baobab and the Guggenheim — a continuous journey with no corridors, only spaces that flow into one another.

01 Gallery

Stolen Cultural Objects Gallery

Six hundred stolen and missing objects from over 50 countries, arranged on a timeline — from a Syrian gold pendant of 120 AD taken from the Palmyra Museum to works still missing today.

 02 Atlas

Regions

Journey across the continents. Explore the stolen objects by region of origin and discover how trafficking affects every part of the world.

 03 Stories of return

Return & Restitution Room

A space of hope that grows with every recovery — a bronze cauldron, a Cycladic figurine, an ancient vase, each told through its journey from theft to homecoming.

 04 Timeline

Historic Timeline

Objects across the periods of history — witnesses to shared memory carried across centuries, now traced through time.

 05 The core

The Baobab

The generative heart of the museum. Cultural objects as the roots of communities — the structure branches into new perspectives as you explore.

 06 You curate

My Gallery

Become a curator. Filter by region, category, date, material or colour and assemble your own personalised gallery in real time.

Audrey Azoulay, UNESCO Director-General

“Behind every stolen work or fragment lies a piece of history, identity and humanity that has been wrenched from its custodians. Our objective is to place these works back in the spotlight, and to restore the right of societies to access their heritage, experience it, and recognise themselves in it.”
