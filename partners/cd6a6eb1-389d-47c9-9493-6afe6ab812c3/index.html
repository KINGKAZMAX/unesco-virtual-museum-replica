The architect

Francis Kéré: an architect serving communities and heritage

UNESCO asked architect Francis Kéré to create the visual identity of the Virtual Museum of Stolen Cultural Objects. The Pritzker Prize winner combined tradition and innovation in a vision highlighting how vital heritage is to individual and community identity and development.

Kéré, born in 1965 in Gando, Burkina Faso, trained first as a carpenter, then studied architecture at Berlin's Technische Universität. He later founded Kéré Architecture and the Kéré Foundation. His career centers on architecture serving communities, with schools, health centres, public spaces, and civic buildings across Africa (Burkina Faso, Benin, Mali, Togo, Kenya, Mozambique). His approach relies on local materials adapted to climate and environment, blending traditional knowledge with elegant and sustainable contemporary architecture.

His honors include the Aga Khan Award for Architecture (2004), the Global Award for Sustainable Architecture (2009), the Pritzker Prize (2022), and the Praemium Imperiale (2023).

Kéré's values and priorities are in line with those of UNESCO

Respect for tradition, sustainability, and community participation. He sees architecture as strengthening social and environmental justice, with projects born from dialogue so inhabitants "become actors, taking ownership of the building" as part of their identity.

His design for the museum draws on the baobab tree, "a monumental tree and symbol of resilience" central to many African communities. He treats cultural property as the "roots" of communities, linking tangible and intangible heritage — both vulnerable to illicit trafficking.

A museum like no other

Stolen objects regain a voice through contextual elements, showing how trafficking strips communities of collective memory, knowledge, and traditions. The project offers an immersive, collective experience inviting the public to help fight trafficking of cultural property.
