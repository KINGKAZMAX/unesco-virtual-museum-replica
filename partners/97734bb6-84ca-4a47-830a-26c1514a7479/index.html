Return & restitution

UNESCO's Role in the Restitution of Cultural Property

The long-standing commitment to return and restitution lies at the heart of UNESCO's mission.

In this debate, the Organisation has been at the forefront of global discussions and advocacy. Since its creation, it has served as a multilateral platform for cooperation, dialogue, and support to Member States on this important issue — one that goes beyond the notion of objects themselves and touches upon questions of identity, spirituality, education, justice and development, particularly through museums.

UNESCO regards every restitution as a meaningful step forward in the protection and restoration of cultural heritage. Restitution allows saving objects from oblivion, enables both scientific progress and communities to reconnect with their heritage, and restores cultural diversity and identity. Restitution also sends a strong signal to traffickers who mutilate heritage — to those who traffic to finance violence, terrorism or extremism.

The 1970 UNESCO Convention on the Means of Prohibiting and Preventing the Illicit Import, Export and Transfer of Ownership of Cultural Property lays down fundamental rules for the return and restitution of cultural property. It emphasizes the importance of international cooperation and provides measures to prevent illicit trafficking. However, this groundbreaking Convention is non-retroactive, and its scope is limited. The 1995 UNIDROIT Convention complements it.

UNESCO promotes the return and restitution, in the context of the 1970 Convention or beyond, in which case Member States act "in the spirit of the 1970 Convention".

In 1978, Amadou Mahtar M'Bow, UNESCO Director-General from 1974 to 1987, issued a powerful Plea for the return of an irreplaceable cultural heritage to those who created it, stating that "the men and women of these countries have the right to recover these cultural assets which are part of their being". His vision led to the creation of the Intergovernmental Committee for Promoting the Return of Cultural Property to its Countries of Origin or its Restitution in Case of Illicit Appropriation, which held its first session in 1980. M'Bow's advocacy marked a shift from bilateral diplomacy to a global ethical imperative, laying the groundwork for UNESCO's leadership in this area.

An international question

While this question was long confined to diplomatic relations as a bilateral issue, a more public international debate has only begun to take shape in recent years. Ethical perceptions have evolved, and there is a tangible shift in cultural and institutional attitudes — whether on the side of national authorities, museum professionals, artists or civil society. In this context, the MONDIACULT Declaration 2022, adopted unanimously by Member States at the UNESCO World Conference on Cultural Policies and Sustainable Development in Mexico in September 2022, gave new impetus to UNESCO's efforts to support its Member States in preserving cultural heritage and cultural rights, by fighting illicit trafficking and promoting an open and inclusive dialogue on return and restitution.

UNESCO has consistently advocated for the fight against illicit trafficking in cultural property and for return and restitution to be better integrated into multilateral forums. One clear result of these advocacy efforts is the explicit inclusion of the issue of return and restitution in the 'Pact for the Future' adopted at the United Nations General Assembly in 2024. This momentum has also been reflected in the attention given to these issues by the G20, to which UNESCO is a privileged partner. The Organization promotes new forms of agreements and cooperation in this field that help build trust and strengthen relationships beyond the return and restitution of objects, fostering deeper cultural and scientific collaboration and strengthening dialogue among governments, institutions, experts, and communities.

UNESCO recognizes how important it is for return and restitution processes to be inclusive

This means involving everyone concerned, including Indigenous peoples, whose knowledge can help identify objects and understand their traditional meanings and uses. The Organization also values the role of museums as active spaces for learning and dialogue. UNESCO supports museums in preserving and documenting collections, which contributes to prevent illicit trafficking. Museums also play a key role in helping returned and restituted objects find their place again in society — by sharing their stories, reviewing museology and raising public awareness, so that communities can reconnect with them.

Working in close cooperation with its partners, UNESCO's multidisciplinary programmatic approach includes technical support, capacity building, research, educational programmes and a holistic approach to museum development — all crucial in the efforts to promote return and restitution.
