Fight illicit trafficking

The 1970 Convention

The 1970 UNESCO Convention on the Means of Prohibiting and Preventing the Illicit Import, Export, and Transfer of Ownership of Cultural Property — a landmark international treaty that has shaped global efforts to safeguard cultural heritage.

Learn more about the 1970 Convention

Adopted on 14 November 1970, the Convention was the first binding legal instrument to recognize the need for coordinated international action to combat cultural property trafficking. At its core, it recognizes that cultural property should be protected from illicit import, export and transfer of ownership. Before its adoption, cultural property often circulated without documentation or regulation, and many countries lacked the legal tools to recover stolen property. The Convention addressed these challenges by creating a framework for cooperation and responsibility among States.

States Parties commit to several key obligations:

  • Preventive Measures: States are required to establish inventories, adopt export certificates, regulate the art trade, and enforce controls on the movement of cultural property.
  • Prohibition of Illicit Import and Return and Restitution Mechanisms: The Convention prohibits the import of cultural property stolen from museums, religious institutions, or similar entities in other States Parties. States have committed to take appropriate steps to recover and return any such cultural property.
  • Education and Awareness: Parties have undertaken to promote public awareness of the value of cultural property and the threat to cultural heritage created by theft, clandestine excavations and illicit exports.

Today, more than 145 countries have ratified the 1970 Convention, making it a cornerstone of the international legal framework for the protection of cultural property. Many States have also adopted detailed national legislation to implement its provisions. These laws are compiled in UNESCO's Database of National Cultural Heritage Laws, which currently contains over 3,000 legal texts from 179 countries.

The Convention also laid the groundwork for subsequent treaties and initiatives

Among them is the 1995 UNIDROIT Convention on Stolen or Illegally Exported Cultural Objects, which addressed important issues of private law left open by the 1970 Convention, such as the notion of acquisition in "good faith" and the time limits for bringing legal claims. Its legacy can also be seen in the United Nations Security Council resolutions concerning looted cultural objects from conflict zones.

The Convention's influence extends well beyond the sphere of public international law. It has informed museum acquisition policies, inspired the adoption of ethical standards in the art market, and fostered a culture of accountability and shared responsibility in safeguarding cultural heritage. It notably influenced the principles laid out in the UNESCO International Code of Ethics for Dealers in Cultural Property: currently under revision, the Code aims to guide the most responsible traders — those actively engaged in combating illicit trafficking by conducting rigorous and systematic due diligence checks.

The organization facilitates cooperation between law enforcement agencies, customs officials, the judiciary, art market, museum professionals, and cultural ministries to ensure that illicit trafficking is detected, disrupted, and prosecuted effectively. Public engagement is another vital component of the Convention's success. UNESCO encourages individuals to become advocates for ethical collecting, provenance research, and respect for cultural diversity.

The 1970 UNESCO Convention remains as relevant today as when it was adopted over five decades ago. It continues to evolve to meet new challenges, such as online trafficking, and threats posed by armed conflict and climate change. Through its enduring principles, the Convention not only preserves cultural property but also strengthens bonds between nations, reinforces cultural identity, and affirms the universal right to access and enjoy humanity's common history.
